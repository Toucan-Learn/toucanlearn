Year 6 student: Ongoing creativity and academic transfer

He thought he was “uncreative”. The evidence said otherwise.

One autistic Year 6 learner arrived with sophisticated ideas and strong systems thinking, but really low confidence in his ability to create or organise them, as well as general expression. A real interest-led approach, and game-development project gave those ideas somewhere to go.

The project was the access route, not the endpoint. Planning, explanation and written structure incrementally increased as his ownership grew.

Baseline Strong ideas, quite weak confidence and creative self-belief

He described himself as “uncreative”. Josh’s early assessment was the opposite: ambitious ideas were already there, but expressing, sequencing and refining them still depended heavily on adult structure.

Target Make the ideas visible and transferable

Use interests and one meaningful project to teach planning, sequencing, iteration and Point–Evidence–Explanation within fun environments—then transfer those skills into academic writing.

Current results Project ownership and secondary-level writing

He now develops and explains original systems within his Pig F1 project, and produces many excellent writing pieces, including Year 8-level History writing while still in Year 6.

The project-to-literacy bridge

Pig F1 made English practical.

The game needed the same things strong writing needs: a clear purpose, precise vocabulary, ordered steps, decisions supported with reasons and enough explanation for another person to follow.

"Present to me about one country that spawned since the end of WW1. Tell me 3 important details!"

Political structure and governance. Yugoslavia was formed after the fallout of World War One in 1918. Up to World War Two, it ran under a monarchy until it was invaded by the Axis powers. After the war, the country transitioned to a communist structure under Josip Broz Tito. The federation consisted of six republics: Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, Serbia and Macedonia.

Breakup and conflicts. Tito died in 1980, and his death led to increased nationalism and tensions among the republics. A sequence of violent conflicts, known as the Yugoslav Wars, eventually led to the split of Yugoslavia. Ethnic tensions defined these wars and resulted in major population displacement.

Legacy. Yugoslavia was a unique experiment in multi-ethnic governance that ultimately faced serious challenges from nationalistic sentiment. Its dissolution created several independent states, each with its own national identity and political challenges. That history still influences the politics and societies of the Balkan region today.

Year 7 student: Ongoing access and catch-up journey

The first target was attempting the work. The next is closing the gap.

For one Year 7 learner with Developmental Language Disorder (DLD) reported as working at year 2 level, conventional tasks often produced one-word answers, “I don’t know”, or complete avoidance. His parent’s immediate concern was whether he could remain successfully in mainstream education.

Toucan treated participation as the baseline for learning, then increased the structure once he had a reason to read, write, calculate and persist.

Baseline Getting him to attempt anything

School-style English and unfamiliar prompts could lose him almost immediately. Low output made it difficult to see what he understood or build consistent practice.

Target Participation, communication and mainstream access

Build enough trust and task tolerance for him to start, stay with and complete increasingly structured English and Maths work.

Current results Broad movement, with the gap now measurable

He completes multi-step digital work, types fuller responses and is attempting practical Maths. Five of seven subject scores rose; in four subjects, the gap to the class average narrowed.

Four individual products, each with a different decision behind it

Starting with his own products gave the learner a reason to name, compare, redesign and persuade. Each concept is shown separately because the evidence is in the choices—not in making them look like one manufactured range.

Origin story · anonymised

The learner who built the method.

One of the first learners Josh supported was a young autistic learner with a PDA profile. He was exceptionally bright, frequently dysregulated and spending much of the school day outside classrooms. School had become almost impossible for him to access.

The question was not “How do I stop this behaviour?” It was “How do I make school somewhere he actually wants to be?”

01 · Relationship Make the day tolerable first

No constant correction, forced worksheets or power struggles. The first goal was simply: let’s get through today together.

02 · Interests Ping pong and music became the curriculum

They were not rewards held back until work was finished. They were genuine routes into trust, conversation, thinking and learning.

03 · Hidden competence The penhold breakthrough

The grip he used to teach Josh table tennis already contained the motor control needed to hold a pencil. The skill existed; it only needed transferring.

04 · A different route The English task became a song

He created the ideas through music and conversation while Josh acted as scribe. The learning objective stayed the same; only the route changed.

Signature moment

“You already know how.”

Writing was described as impossible, yet the learner had taught Josh a precise penhold grip months earlier. Connecting the two did not teach a new movement. It helped him recognise an ability he already possessed.

The music room

Creativity was never the barrier

They headed to the drums and guitars because he wanted to play. Later, an untouched English task became songwriting: ideas, improvised lines and laughter, with Josh writing as he created. The barrier was transcription—not imagination or intelligence.

Relationship before curriculum Trust before challenge Interests before compliance Adapt the route, not the expectation Behaviour communicates unmet needs Look for hidden competence Build self-belief through real success Let success create motivation

Toucan Learn did not begin with a business plan. It began with one learner, one table-tennis bat and the belief that ability often appears when we change the route instead of lowering the destination.
