Ongoing writing and independence journey

From needing the structure held for him to setting, organising and explaining his own work.

One autistic learner began with strong ideas but relied on an adult to hold the writing structure in mind. Across ongoing support, that shifted into self-initiated guide writing, timed reflection and fuller explanations written for a reader.

The route stayed interest-led. The academic demand increased as his independence did.

Starting point Ideas needed external structure

He could explain complex thinking, but needed prompts to organise detail, evaluation and a clear ending.

Access route Purposeful writing through projects

A game guide gave him a real audience and a reason to group, refine and explain information.

Observable shift Self-set, increasingly independent work

He began extending writing outside lessons, returning with new sections and taking ownership of the structure.

How the progression showed up

Not a vague claim of “better writing”: four observable changes recorded across the work.

Structured explanation Detail moved into evaluation

With an explicit frame, he produced a focused comparison with one idea per sentence and a clear evaluative ending.

Self-initiation Extended his guide between lessons

On three occasions he independently continued substantial writing, then brought it back to refine and organise.

Writing stamina Independent work under light time limits

Short recall tasks progressed from verbal discussion to written responses completed independently with gentle time boundaries.

Reader awareness More context, evidence and explanation

Using a simple Point–Evidence–Explanation frame, he began making project decisions understandable to someone seeing the work fresh.

He said he was really enjoying the lessons and did not want anything changed.

Ongoing engagement and independence journey

From one-word avoidance to sustained work across writing, Maths and digital projects.

For one learner with a confirmed diagnosis of Developmental Language Disorder (DLD), progress first meant staying with a task long enough for learning to happen. Practical digital projects created a route into reading, writing, reasoning and explanation without disguising the challenge involved.

Engagement was treated as the starting condition for learning—not the final goal. Structure and demand were increased once that foundation existed.

Starting point Formal prompts often led to minimal output

Answers could be one word, and unfamiliar or school-style tasks could quickly lose him.

Access route Real purposes for reading and writing

Game-building, design, comics and practical problem-solving made communication useful in the moment.

Observable shift Longer attention, fuller output, more ownership

He began persisting through multi-step tasks, correcting mistakes and producing work more independently.

Concrete evidence from sessions

The progress appears across different tasks, rather than depending on one unusually good lesson.

Technical persistence Completed an instruction-heavy software tutorial

He followed the full sequence, backtracked after errors and stayed engaged despite lag and difficult menus.

Written output Six self-typed sentences in one short task

A brief, timed activity produced several independent ideas where earlier responses had often stayed minimal.

Sequenced expression Created a multi-panel comic

He generated the plot himself, organised a beginning, middle and end, then combined drawings with short captions.

Functional transfer More independent tools and practical Maths

He navigated, searched and used design tools after modelling, while projects embedded number comparison and missing-number reasoning.

Where the approach began

The learner who built the method.

One of the first learners I ever supported was an autistic learner with a PDA profile who was finding school almost impossible to access. The question that changed the work was not “How do I stop this behaviour?” but “How do I make school somewhere he actually wants to be?”

Relationship came first. Ping pong, music and conversation became routes into learning—not rewards offered after it.

01 Relationship before curriculum

Trust made meaningful challenge possible.

02 Look for hidden competence

A penhold table-tennis grip revealed the motor control needed for a pencil.

03 Change the route, not the goal

Songwriting unlocked the same English objective a worksheet could not reach.

04 Let success build belief

Ability became visible once the learner could recognise it in himself.

What one learner revealed

The barrier was not intelligence. It was the route he was being asked to take.

  • Trust before challenge and regulation before correction
  • Interests used as the curriculum, not a reward for compliance
  • Existing strengths transferred into seemingly impossible tasks
  • The same learning destination reached through a different route

When the route changed, his ability became visible.
