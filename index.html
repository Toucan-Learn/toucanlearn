Ongoing literacy journey

From supported ideas to sustained, higher-level writing.

Across nine months, one KS2 learner moved from short, heavily prompted responses into ambitious English work that now demands real planning, stamina and thought.

Progress was built through projects that mattered to him, with structure added gradually as trust and tolerance for challenge grew.

What changed

The learner’s interests remained the route in; the academic demand around them became progressively more sophisticated.

  • Longer pieces with clearer planning, sequencing and description
  • More willingness to explain choices and revise ideas
  • Increasing independence without removing useful scaffolding
  • Workload adjusted when two demanding English sessions became too much

Where the approach began

The learner who built the method.

One of the first learners I ever supported was a 12-year-old autistic learner with a PDA profile. The question that changed the work was not “How do I stop this behaviour?” but “How do I make school somewhere he actually wants to be?”

Relationship came first. Ping pong, music and conversation became routes into learning—not rewards offered after it.

01 Relationship before curriculum

Trust made meaningful challenge possible.

02 Look for hidden competence

A penhold table-tennis grip revealed the motor control needed for a pencil.

03 Change the route, not the goal

Songwriting unlocked the same English objective a worksheet could not reach.

04 Let success build belief

Ability became visible once the learner could recognise it in himself.

What one learner revealed

The barrier was not intelligence. It was the route he was being asked to take.

  • Trust before challenge and regulation before correction
  • Interests used as the curriculum, not a reward for compliance
  • Existing strengths transferred into seemingly impossible tasks
  • The same learning destination reached through a different route

When the route changed, his ability became visible.
